I am struggling with an assignment. I have to write a plan and it's taking me forever. I exhibit clear procrastination behaviour and I am looking for a way to get going and finish the plan.

So, I came up with the idea of declaring it would take me another 32 hours to finish writing the plan and than focus on the hours I am putting in, assuming I will have finished the plan once I put in another 32 hours. This would solve one of my problems, which is that I am not even starting to work on it, just keep postponoing the actual sitting down and working on it.

This idea lead me to revisit [the pomodoro technique](https://francescocirillo.com/pages/pomodoro-technique).  The idea is that you follow these steps:

1. Pick a task you want to work on
2. Set the pomodoro (a simple timer) to 25 minutes
3. Work on the task  until the time runs out 
4. When the pomodoro rings, put a checkmark on paper
5. Take a short break
6. Every 4 pomodori, take a longer break

The underlying idea is a combination of focused, uninterrupted work and evaluation of your progress and way of working.

By using this pomodoro technique and recording the number of sessions a project or task takes, you get a better idea of your own productivity (while increasing it at the same time). There are supposedly about 8 to 10 pomodori in a day. In a way, that's shockingly little, but at the other hand it would be great to be able to work in a focused way for such a long time each and every day.

As is pretty usual for me, there was quite a distraction flowing over me. Between these two paragraphs there has been a time-gap of about half an hour (yes indeed, equal to a pomodoro including a break...).
I wanted to see if there was a Workflowy way to work with the pomodoro technique and well, there is. So, I have been tinkering around with color-coding and css styles for tags and I have to see, there are quite some interesting possibilities.

So, let's say I want to learn about my productivity and progress, I would start out with determining waht I would want to track. I think thta would be how much time (so how many pomodori) I am spending on a project (the reason for measuring is two-fold: to determine if I am charging enough to my client and on the other hand if I am putting in the necessary hours and effort).

As you can see, I run the risk of creating too many different projects, and by doing so I decrease the chance of successfully implementing this way of working. It doesn't really matter, as long as I realise I don't have to do everything in chunks of pomodori and I also don't need to keep track of everything I do. As can be seen in the example of Frank Degenaar, there can be days without doing anything useful. And, also a good thing to remember, creating a habit takes time. So just start with the whisky project, that's the one for which you need this help. If this helps you finish that task/project, it's already a success. If it happens to help you work more efficiently/effectively/focused, that's a bonus.

So, being able to see the actual amounts of succesful pomodori per project per day, we need a calendar. [Here's my calendar in Workflowy](https://workflowy.com/#/b80269ea1c15) showing the number of pomodori per project per day. Simply typing #wh next to a date shows I have worked on the Whisky project for one pomodoro. Having done three gets noted #wh #wh #wh. In combination with the CSS in the Stylus stylesheet, this shows three dark-red boxes. 

This is a good starting-point. Some reminders to increase the chances of this working for you:
- don't break the chain. Work on this project everyday until it is finished
- break down a project in tasks. A task is something you can get done, a project isn't until you break it up in tasks.
- don't forget the evaluation part
- take away distractions. Before starting a pomodoro, make sure your desk is empty, except for a notebook to write down anything that is about to dsitract you.
- When you think about something else to do, write it down on a piece of paper.
